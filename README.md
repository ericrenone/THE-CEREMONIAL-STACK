# THE-CEREMONIAL-STACK

**Why the Newly Promoted Executive Director Manages the Shape of the Work Rather Than the Work, Why the Job Posting Asks for Comfort With an Ambiguity the Poster Produced, Why Certifications Stack Where Insight Does Not, Why "Best Practice" Is the Prior Regime Wearing a Badge, and Why Managers Who Share a Gap Hire Together to Hide It**

Eric Ren · ERI Labs · Jersey City, New Jersey · October 2026 · github.com/ericrenone

---

## Foundations

Katz (1955) proposed that an administrator needs three skills — technical, human, and conceptual — and that the technical skill matters less at each higher level of the organization. The proposition became the structural justification for promoting people away from the work.

Collins and Evans (2007) distinguished contributory expertise, the ability to do the work of a domain, from interactional expertise, the ability to speak its language fluently without being able to do it. The second can be acquired by immersion in conversation alone and is indistinguishable from the first in conversation.

Meyer and Rowan (1977) established that organizations adopt formal structures for legitimacy rather than efficiency, that these structures are ceremonial, and that they are decoupled from the technical core so that the ceremony can be performed without disturbing the work.

DiMaggio and Powell (1983) established that organizations come to resemble one another through coercive, mimetic, and normative pressure, and that under uncertainty they imitate whatever appears legitimate.

Benson, Li, and Shue (2019) found, in a large sample of firms, that employees are promoted on the basis of performance in their current role and that this performance does not predict, and is sometimes inversely related to, their performance as managers.

Rivera (2012) found that hiring in elite professional firms is cultural matching: evaluators select candidates who resemble themselves and experience the resemblance as merit.

Budner (1962) operationalized intolerance of ambiguity as a personality trait. The trait has since migrated from the psychology laboratory to the job posting, where its absence is now requested as a qualification.

## I. The Promotion That Changed Regime

Consider the executive director promoted in the second quarter of 2026. The promotion was earned. For eleven years this person delivered: ran programs that shipped, kept stakeholders aligned, absorbed escalations, produced the quarterly narrative that leadership could carry upward. The promotion packet documented a record of execution in a function the organization understood. The role it conferred is a role the organization does not yet understand: head of AI workflow transformation for a division of several thousand people.

The record that produced the promotion was compiled under one regime. The role is in another. Under the first regime the work was legible to a generalist — a program was a set of workstreams, a workstream was a set of deliverables, a deliverable was a document or a release, and the manager's contribution was to keep the set coherent and the stakeholders calm. Under the second regime the work is a set of interactions between people, models, tools, and data pipelines whose behavior is probabilistic, whose failure modes are novel, and whose design choices — which model, which context, which evaluation, which boundary between automated and human judgment — determine whether the workflow produces value or produces a convincing imitation of value. The manager's contribution under the second regime cannot be coherence and calm alone. It requires the ability to see whether the thing being built is the right thing, and that ability is technical.

The executive director does not have it. This is not a character flaw. It is the predictable output of a promotion system that selects on prior-regime performance during a regime change. Benson, Li, and Shue (2019) documented the mechanism in ordinary conditions: firms promote their best performers into management and the best performers are not the best managers, because the skills are different and the promotion signal measures the wrong one. Under a regime change the mechanism is amplified. The skill the promotion measured — execution in a legible domain — is not merely different from the skill the role requires. It was calibrated to a domain that no longer exists in the form the skill was fitted to.

The organizational response to this condition is the subject of this document. It is not the response one would design. It is the response that emerges, with structural regularity, from a layer of managers who hold authority over work they cannot evaluate, who must produce the appearance of governing it, and who are evaluated by a layer above them that cannot evaluate it either. The response has four visible components, and each will be named and derived.

The first is the stack: the accumulation of certifications, frameworks, and ceremonies — Agile, SAFe, product-operating-model badges, AI-for-leaders programs, responsible-AI governance charters — that govern the form of the work without touching its content. The second is the clause: the migration of the manager's own inability to specify the work into the job posting, where it appears as a requirement that the candidate be comfortable with ambiguity. The third is the coalition: the convergence of managers who share the gap into hiring, promotion, and evaluation groups that select for one another and against the candidate who would expose them. The fourth is the drift: the progressive movement of the manager, with each year of calendar tenure, from the work to the shape of the work, until the only thing the manager can manage is the ceremony.

The document's thesis is that these four components are one phenomenon. The manager who cannot see the work builds a layer of structure that can be seen instead, hires people who share the layer, and describes the gap between the layer and the work as a property of the environment rather than of the manager. The layer is the Ceremonial Stack. It sits on top of the technical stack the way a user interface sits on top of a system: it presents controls, it reports status, it produces the experience of operation. It is not the system. And under an AI transition, where the system is the strategy, managing the interface is not managing anything.

## II. The Two Expertises and the Interactional Ceiling

Collins and Evans (2007) made a distinction that organizational life had always relied on without naming. Contributory expertise is the capacity to contribute to a domain — to do the work, make the judgments, produce the artifacts that practitioners recognize as competent. Interactional expertise is the capacity to converse fluently in the domain's language — to follow its arguments, use its vocabulary correctly, ask questions that practitioners find sensible, and pass, in conversation, as a member. Interactional expertise is real expertise. It is acquired through sustained immersion in the talk of a domain, and it is not trivial. But it is acquired without doing the work, and its signature limitation is that the holder cannot tell, from the inside, where their interactional expertise ends and contributory expertise would have to begin.

The manager promoted away from the work is, by construction, an interactional expert in the domain they manage. They were once contributory — the eleven years of execution — but contributory expertise in a prior regime converts, under a regime change, into interactional expertise in the new one. The executive director can speak about AI workflows. They have sat in the meetings, read the decks, absorbed the vocabulary of agents, retrieval, evaluation, guardrails, orchestration. They can ask whether a workflow has been evaluated. They cannot tell whether the evaluation was adequate, because that judgment requires contributory expertise: knowing what a good evaluation looks like, what it would miss, what the model's failure distribution implies for the workflow's design.

The Interactional Ceiling is the altitude in an organization above which no one holds contributory expertise in the work the organization is transforming. Below the ceiling, decisions about the work are made or at least checked by people who could do it. Above the ceiling, decisions are made by people whose knowledge of the work is entirely linguistic. The ceiling is not fixed. In a stable regime it sits high, because the generalists above it were once practitioners and their contributory expertise, though stale, still covers most of what matters. Under a regime change the ceiling drops, sometimes several levels in a year, because the practitioners' prior contributory expertise no longer covers the new work and converts to interactional expertise in it.

The executive director sits above the ceiling. So do the VPs who promoted them and the managing director who sponsors the transformation. Below the ceiling are the engineers, data scientists, and the handful of product people who have actually built an AI workflow and watched it fail. The ceiling runs between them, and the decisive feature of the organization is that authority runs downward across it while contributory expertise runs upward only as far as the ceiling permits.

Three consequences follow from the ceiling.

The first is that evaluation above the ceiling is evaluation of presentation. The executive director cannot assess whether the workflow is well-designed. They can assess whether the person presenting it is confident, whether the deck is coherent, whether the timeline is credible, whether the stakeholder map is complete, and whether the vocabulary is current. These are the dimensions on which interactional expertise can discriminate, and they are the dimensions on which the work will be judged. The work that is best-designed and the work that is best-presented are not the same work, and above the ceiling only the second is visible.

The second is that the ceiling is invisible from above. The interactional expert cannot see the boundary of their own expertise because the boundary is defined in terms of a capacity they lack. Kruger and Dunning (1999) established the general form: competence in a domain is required to assess competence in that domain, so those with the least have the least capacity to detect it. The ceiling is the organizational instance. The executive director experiences their understanding of AI workflows as adequate because the test of adequacy — could you build one, could you tell a good one from a bad one — is a test they cannot administer to themselves. What they experience instead is fluency, and fluency feels like understanding.

The third is that the ceiling converts the organization's structural problem into a staffing problem. The organization has authority above the ceiling and expertise below it. The structural solution would be to raise the ceiling — to promote contributory experts into the authority layer — or to lower the authority — to push decision rights about the work down to where the expertise is. Neither happens, because both would require the layer above the ceiling to acknowledge that the ceiling exists. What happens instead is that the layer above the ceiling attempts to hire its way across it: to acquire, in subordinate positions, the contributory expertise it lacks, while retaining the authority to evaluate what the subordinates produce. This is the Absorber Hire, treated below. Its structural property is that it does not raise the ceiling. It installs expertise beneath it and leaves the evaluation problem exactly where it was.

## III. The Katz Inversion

Katz's (1955) three-skill model has structured management development for seventy years. Technical skill — proficiency in the specific activities of the work — dominates at the supervisory level. Human skill — the ability to work with and through people — matters at every level. Conceptual skill — the ability to see the enterprise as a whole and the relationships among its parts — dominates at the top. The implication, drawn by every promotion committee since, is that technical skill is something one grows out of. The executive who still wants to understand the work is, in this view, failing to develop. The executive who has transcended it is the one ready for the next level.

The model was correct under its own conditions. Katz was describing organizations whose technical core was stable: a plant that would make the same product by the same process for a generation, a bank whose underwriting would not change in kind for decades. When the technical core is stable, the executive does not need to understand it, because the understanding is embedded in the routines and the routines are not going to change. Conceptual skill is what remains to be exercised, and it is exercised over a technical substrate that can be taken as given.

The AI transition removes the condition. The technical core is not stable. It is the thing being transformed, and the transformation is the strategy. The question of which workflows to automate, how far, with what models, under what evaluation, with what boundary between machine and human judgment — this is not a technical detail to be delegated downward from a conceptual vantage. It is the conceptual question. The enterprise-as-a-whole that Katz's top executive is supposed to see is, in 2026, an enterprise whose parts are being rewired by a technology, and seeing the whole requires seeing the rewiring, and seeing the rewiring is technical.

This is the Katz Inversion: under a technological regime change, the relationship between altitude and technical skill reverses. Technical understanding is not less necessary at the top; it is more necessary, because the top is where the decisions about the transformation are made, and those decisions are technical decisions dressed as strategic ones. The executive who has grown out of technical skill, as the model instructed, has grown out of the capacity to make the decisions the role now requires.

The inversion produces a specific pathology. The organization's development machinery — its promotion criteria, its leadership programs, its executive assessment frameworks — is built on the uninverted model. It continues to select for conceptual skill as the model defines it: the ability to see relationships among parts, to think strategically, to communicate upward. It continues to treat technical depth as a junior attribute that a candidate for senior roles should have moved past. The machinery therefore produces, with each promotion cycle, exactly the executive the inverted environment cannot use: conceptually fluent, humanly skilled, and technically hollow in the one domain where the technical and the conceptual have merged.

The executive produced by this machinery is not aware of the inversion, because the machinery that produced them is the machinery that defines competence for them. They were told, at every stage, that moving away from the work was development. They moved away from it. They were rewarded. The reward confirmed the model. The model says they are ready. The environment says they cannot see the thing they are responsible for. The two messages cannot both be processed, and the first has eleven years of reinforcement behind it.

What the executive does with this condition is the Ceremonial Stack.

## IV. The Ceremonial Stack

Meyer and Rowan (1977) observed that organizations adopt formal structures — departments, titles, procedures, programs — not because the structures make the organization more efficient but because the structures make the organization look like what a legitimate organization is supposed to look like. The structures are ceremonial. They are performed for an audience of regulators, investors, peers, and the organization's own members, and their function is to signal conformity to institutional expectations. Because they are ceremonial, they are decoupled from the technical core: the organization performs the structure in its formal reporting while the actual work proceeds on its own logic, and the decoupling is what allows both to continue. Inspection of the ceremony is avoided, or ritualized, so that the gap between the ceremony and the work is never measured.

The Ceremonial Stack is the organizational structure a manager above the Interactional Ceiling assembles in order to govern work they cannot see. It has layers, and each layer is a formal structure of the kind Meyer and Rowan described.

The base layer is the process framework. Agile in its packaged forms, SAFe most prominently, supplies a complete vocabulary and ritual calendar for the governance of technical work by non-technical managers: program increments, release trains, ceremonies, cadences, backlogs, story points, velocity, burndown. The framework's design purpose — this is not a criticism; it is a description — is to render technical work legible to management without requiring management to understand it. The work is decomposed into units that have a size but no content. The units are arranged in a cadence that has a rhythm but no direction. The manager governs the size and the rhythm. The content and the direction remain below the ceiling.

The second layer is the certification. The manager who has adopted the framework certifies in it, and the certification is the credential by which the manager's authority over the work is legitimated. Collins (1979) described the credential society as one in which formal credentials substitute for demonstrated competence as the currency of occupational access; the certification layer of the Ceremonial Stack is the credential society applied to the governance of a domain the credential holder cannot practice. The certifications stack. A manager will hold a scaled-agile credential, a product-ownership credential, a project-management credential, and — the 2026 addition — an AI-for-leaders or AI-literacy credential, each of which certifies the holder's fluency in a vocabulary about the work. None certifies the capacity to do it. The stacking is the signal: the manager's investment in credentials about the work is the manager's substitute for the investment in the work that the Katz model told them to abandon.

The third layer is the governance apparatus. The transformation office, the center of excellence, the steering committee, the responsible-AI council, the architecture review board, the model risk committee. Each is a formal structure that produces documents — charters, standards, review templates, risk taxonomies — and each is staffed, at the top, by people above the ceiling. The apparatus produces the appearance of oversight. What it oversees is the ceremony: whether the workflow has been through the review, whether the template was completed, whether the risk taxonomy was applied. Whether the workflow is any good is a question the apparatus cannot ask, because its members cannot answer it.

The fourth layer is the metric. Form Metrics, treated in the next section, are the quantitative surface of the stack: the numbers the ceremony produces about itself.

The Ceremonial Stack has three properties that distinguish it from ordinary management structure.

It is content-blind by design. Every element of the stack operates on the form of the work — its size, cadence, documentation, review status — and none operates on its content. This is not a defect of the stack. It is what allows the stack to be operated by someone above the ceiling. A structure that required judgment about content could not be operated by an interactional expert, and the stack exists precisely to be operable by one.

It is decoupled from the technical core, and the decoupling is stable. The engineers below the ceiling learn to feed the stack: to size the units, attend the ceremonies, complete the templates, produce the metrics. They do this at a cost — the cost of translating real work into ceremonial units — and in exchange the stack leaves the real work alone. Brown and Duguid (1991) described the gap between canonical practice, the work as the organization's formal structures describe it, and noncanonical practice, the work as it is actually done. The Ceremonial Stack is the canonical layer. The work happens in the noncanonical layer beneath it. The two are connected by a translation the engineers perform and the managers do not know is being performed.

It is self-legitimating. Each layer of the stack validates the others. The framework requires the certification; the certification validates the governance apparatus; the apparatus produces the metrics; the metrics demonstrate that the framework is working. DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) mimetic isomorphism closes the loop at the sector level: every peer organization has the same stack, and the stack's ubiquity is taken as evidence of its necessity. The manager who questioned the stack would be questioning the sector's consensus, and the manager is above the ceiling, so they have no independent ground from which to question it.

Under an AI transition the Ceremonial Stack acquires a fifth layer and a specific failure mode. The fifth layer is the AI-specific ceremony: the prompt library, the use-case intake form, the AI readiness assessment, the model inventory, the responsible-AI checklist, the agent governance framework. These are the stack's attempt to extend its content-blind governance to a technology whose content is the entire question. The failure mode is that the AI transition, unlike the software transitions the stack was built on, does not have a stable form. A software deliverable could be sized and scheduled because its behavior was deterministic and its scope could be specified in advance. An AI workflow's behavior is probabilistic, its scope emerges from evaluation, and its design is iterative in a way that story points cannot capture. The stack, applied to this work, does not merely fail to see the content. It actively misdescribes the form, imposing a cadence and a decomposition the work cannot follow, and the engineers beneath the ceiling spend an increasing share of their capacity performing a translation that is no longer approximately true.

## V. Form Metrics and the Decoupled Core

A manager above the ceiling needs numbers. Numbers are the medium through which the manager reports upward, and the layer above is also above the ceiling, so the numbers must be interpretable without understanding the work. The Ceremonial Stack produces such numbers in quantity: velocity, story points completed, sprint commitment ratios, cycle time, backlog health, percent of use cases through intake, number of models in the inventory, percent of workflows with a completed risk assessment, training-completion rates, adoption rates measured as logins.

These are Form Metrics: quantities that measure the ceremony's own activity and are reported as measurements of the work. Each has a precise referent — the number of templates completed is the number of templates completed — and each is taken, above the ceiling, as a proxy for something it does not measure. Velocity is taken as productivity. Use cases through intake are taken as transformation progress. Risk assessments completed are taken as risk managed. Adoption measured as logins is taken as adoption.

The pathology is Goodhart's, in the form Strathern (1997) gave it: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. But the Ceremonial Stack's version is more specific. The Form Metric was never a measure of the work; it was a measure of the ceremony, and it was adopted as a target because it was the only quantity the layer above the ceiling could read. The decoupling between metric and work is not a corruption that set in after the metric became a target. It was present at adoption, because the metric was selected for its legibility to people who could not read the work, and legibility to such people is exactly the property that guarantees the metric does not measure the work.

Below the ceiling, Form Metrics are a tax. Engineers learn which numbers the stack rewards and produce them, at a cost in capacity diverted from the work to the ceremony. The cost is invisible above the ceiling because the metric that would reveal it — the share of engineering capacity spent on translation rather than production — is not a Form Metric. It is a content metric, and no one above the ceiling can specify it.

Above the ceiling, Form Metrics are the only evidence that exists. The executive director's quarterly review to the managing director is a review of Form Metrics, because those are the numbers both parties can interpret. The review produces a shared sense that the transformation is on track, measured in intake volume, template completion, and training rates. Whether any workflow has produced value — whether the division is doing anything with AI that it could not do before, at a cost that makes sense — is not on the slide, because it cannot be put on the slide by anyone in the room.

The decoupling has a terminal form. Power (1997) described the audit society as one in which verification rituals proliferate because they produce comfort rather than assurance: the audit checks that the process was followed, not that the outcome was achieved, and the checking of the process becomes the organization's substitute for knowing the outcome. The Ceremonial Stack under AI is an audit society in miniature. The responsible-AI checklist is completed. The model inventory is current. The review board has met. The governance is verifiably in place. And whether the workflows the governance governs do what they are supposed to do is a question the governance cannot answer and, having been verified, no longer needs to.

## VI. The Ambiguity Clause

Read the job posting. The role is a senior product or program position in an AI transformation function at a large institution. The qualifications list the usual credentials and the usual years. And then, near the top of the "who you are" section, a sentence that has become so standard it is no longer noticed: the candidate must be comfortable with ambiguity, thrive in ambiguity, navigate ambiguity, be energized by ambiguity, operate effectively in ambiguous environments.

The sentence is a request for a personality trait. Budner (1962) operationalized intolerance of ambiguity as a disposition: the tendency to perceive ambiguous situations as threatening. The job posting asks for its absence. It asks the candidate to be the kind of person who does not experience ambiguous situations as threatening, and it presents this as a requirement of the work.

The framework's claim is that the ambiguity is not in the work. It is in the gap between the manager and the work, and the clause is the manager's export of that gap into the candidate's personality.

Consider what the manager above the ceiling can and cannot do when writing the posting. They can specify the form: the level, the reporting line, the stakeholders, the ceremonies the role will attend, the credentials it should hold. They cannot specify the content: what the role will actually build, what the technical constraints are, what good output looks like, what the first ninety days should produce. They cannot specify the content because they cannot see it. The posting therefore has a shape — a complete form specification wrapped around an empty content specification — and the emptiness has to be accounted for. The clause accounts for it. The emptiness is renamed ambiguity, ambiguity is attributed to the environment, and the candidate is asked to be the kind of person who can tolerate what the manager cannot specify.

This is Ambiguity Externalization: the conversion of a manager's inability to specify work into a stated property of the work and then into a required disposition of the worker. It has three functions.

The first is face. Goffman's (1955) face-work governs the posting as it governs the interview. A manager who wrote "we do not know what this role should build because we do not understand the technology well enough to say" would be making a face-threatening disclosure, upward and outward. "Comfortable with ambiguity" makes the same disclosure in a form that preserves face: the uncertainty is in the world, the role is challenging, and the candidate who cannot handle it is the one with the deficiency.

The second is pre-emptive attribution. If the role fails — if the hire cannot produce value because the role was never specified well enough to be producible — the clause has already assigned the failure. The candidate was told the environment was ambiguous. The candidate said they could handle it. The candidate could not. The manager's inability to specify the role never appears in the account, because the clause converted it into a trait the candidate claimed and lacked.

The third is selection. The clause selects. The candidate who reads it and asks, in the interview, what specifically the role will build and how success will be measured, is demonstrating intolerance of ambiguity. The candidate who reads it and says they love ambiguous environments and figuring things out as they go is demonstrating fit. The first candidate is asking the manager to specify what the manager cannot specify; the second is agreeing to absorb the gap. The clause selects for the second. It selects, that is, for candidates who will not expose the ceiling by asking where it is.

The clause has become ubiquitous because the condition that produces it has become ubiquitous. Every large institution in 2026 has a transformation function staffed above the ceiling, every such function is posting roles whose content the posters cannot specify, and the clause is the sector's shared solution to the shared problem. Its ubiquity is read, by candidates and by the managers who copy it from one another's postings, as evidence that AI work is inherently ambiguous. Some of it is. Most of the ambiguity the clause refers to is not a property of AI. It is a property of the distance between the posting's author and the thing the posting is for.

The clause has a diagnostic use for the candidate below the ceiling. Where "comfortable with ambiguity" appears alongside a detailed form specification — level, ceremonies, credentials, stakeholders — and an absent content specification — no statement of what will be built, by what standard, against what constraint — the clause is a reliable marker that the role sits beneath a manager who cannot see the work. The candidate who takes the role is taking a position whose content they will have to define themselves, whose definition will be evaluated by someone who cannot evaluate it, and whose success will be measured in Form Metrics. That is not ambiguity. It is the Absorber Hire, and the candidate should know it before accepting.

## VII. The Best-Practice Shield and the Borrowed Horizon

Two phrases do the heavy lifting in any meeting above the ceiling. The first is "best practice." The second is some version of "where the industry is going." Both are substitutes for judgment, and both substitute in the same direction: away from the organization's own situation and toward an external authority whose content the speaker has not evaluated.

"Best practice" is the prior regime wearing a badge. A practice becomes "best" by having been adopted widely enough, by legitimate enough organizations, for long enough that its adoption no longer requires justification. The process by which this happens is DiMaggio and Powell's normative and mimetic isomorphism: consultancies codify what the leading firms did, the codification is taught, the taught practice is adopted by firms seeking legitimacy, and the adoption is cited as further evidence that the practice is best. By the time a practice is "best," it describes what worked in the conditions under which it was codified — conditions which, under a regime change, have passed. The AI best practices circulating in 2026 — center-of-excellence models, use-case intake funnels, maturity assessments, responsible-AI frameworks — were codified between 2023 and 2025, in a regime of chat interfaces and retrieval pilots, and they are being applied to a regime of autonomous agents and probabilistic workflows that the codifiers had not seen.

The Best-Practice Shield is the invocation of codified practice as a substitute for the judgment the invoker cannot exercise. A manager above the ceiling, asked why the transformation is structured as it is, answers that the structure follows best practice. The answer is unfalsifiable in the room: no one above the ceiling can assess whether the practice fits the situation, and the practice's legitimacy is the only thing on which everyone present can agree. The shield has the structure of Argyris's (1991) defensive routine: it prevents the examination of the manager's reasoning by placing the reasoning outside the manager, in an authority that cannot be questioned without questioning the sector.

The shield's cost is Abrahamson's (1996) management fashion cycle applied to a transition that moves faster than the cycle. Management fashions have a lifecycle — emergence, codification, diffusion, saturation, decline — and the lifecycle historically ran over years. The AI transition is producing fashions with a lifecycle of quarters: the prompt-engineering center of excellence, the AI champions network, the agentic transformation office. Each is codified as best practice at the moment of peak diffusion, which is the moment the frontier has moved past it. The manager who adopts best practice is adopting the sector's consensus from two quarters ago, and the manager cannot tell, because the consensus is the only thing they can see.

The second phrase names the Borrowed Horizon. A manager above the ceiling is expected to have a vision for the transformation — a view of what the division's work will look like in three years and how it will get there. Vision requires contributory understanding: one cannot envision a future built on a technology one cannot evaluate. The manager therefore imports a vision. The sources are vendor roadmaps, consultant decks, analyst reports, and the keynotes of the three or four technology companies whose products the division has licensed. The imported vision is presented as the manager's own — "where we're taking this" — and it is, in content, the vendor's product roadmap with the division's logo on the slide.

The Borrowed Horizon has a structural consequence that the manager cannot see. The vendor's roadmap is a description of what the vendor intends to sell. The division's needs are a description of what the division's work requires. The two overlap where the vendor has correctly anticipated the market and diverge everywhere else, and the manager who has adopted the vendor's roadmap as the division's vision has no independent position from which to see the divergence. Khurana (2002) described the market for charismatic CEOs as one in which boards, unable to evaluate the substance of candidates, selected on the appearance of vision and imported the vision from the candidate. The Borrowed Horizon is the same mechanism one level down: the manager, unable to generate a vision, imports it from the vendor, and the layer above the manager, unable to evaluate the vision, accepts it as evidence that the manager has one.

The two phrases combine into the manager's complete operating stance. Where the organization should be going is borrowed from the vendor. How it should get there is borrowed from best practice. What remains for the manager to supply is the ceremony — the cadence, the governance, the metrics — and that is what the Ceremonial Stack provides. The manager governs the form of a journey whose destination and route were both supplied from outside, and experiences the governance as leadership.

## VIII. The Gap Coalition

Managers above the ceiling do not sit there alone. They sit there together, and the togetherness is not incidental. It is the mechanism by which the ceiling is maintained.

Rivera (2012) studied hiring in elite law, consulting, and banking firms and found that evaluators selected for cultural similarity — candidates who shared their own backgrounds, interests, and self-presentation — and that they experienced the selection as merit. The candidates who resembled the evaluators seemed, to the evaluators, like the strong ones. McPherson, Smith-Lovin, and Cook (2001) documented the general principle: homophily, the tendency to form ties with similar others, structures networks of every kind, and the similarity on which ties form includes shared competences and shared gaps.

The Gap Coalition is the structure that forms when managers who share a technical gap in the domain they govern hire, promote, and evaluate one another. It forms for three reasons, and the three compound.

The first reason is ordinary homophily. The managers above the ceiling share a background — program management, change management, business operations, consulting — and a vocabulary, and they recognize one another as competent because competence, to each of them, looks like the other. The engineer who presents for a senior role in the transformation function does not look like them. The engineer's competence is in a register they cannot read, the engineer's self-presentation does not match theirs, and the engineer seems, in the interview, less strategic, less polished, more narrowly technical. The evaluators select the candidate who resembles them. The resemblance is the gap.

The second reason is threat. Tesser's (1988) self-evaluation maintenance model predicts that when a proximate other outperforms the self on a self-relevant dimension, the self reduces closeness to the other. A manager above the ceiling who hired a contributory expert into a peer position would be installing, at close range, someone whose understanding of the work exceeds the manager's own in the domain the manager is responsible for. Every meeting would be a comparison. Every decision would be an occasion on which the expert could see what the manager could not. The manager does not need to consciously calculate this. The manager needs only to find the expert, in the interview, a less compelling candidate than the one who resembles the manager — and Tesser's model guarantees that the manager will.

The third reason is collective insurance. The managers above the ceiling are each, individually, exposed: if the layer above them acquired the capacity to evaluate the work, each would be revealed as governing a ceremony. The exposure is shared, and shared exposure produces mutual protection. The managers validate one another's Form Metrics, endorse one another's best practices, co-sign one another's borrowed horizons, and present to the layer above as a consensus. The consensus is the coalition's product. It converts a set of individually unevaluable claims into a collectively credible one, and it ensures that no single member can be questioned without questioning the group.

The coalition has a specific relationship to hiring that produces the phenomenon the title of this document names: hiring around the gap. The coalition cannot hire the gap closed, because closing it would mean installing contributory experts in the coalition's own layer, which the three mechanisms above prevent. The coalition instead hires around the gap: it recruits contributory expertise into positions beneath the ceiling, structured so that the expertise is available to the coalition without being able to evaluate it. This is the Absorber Hire. The absorber is a senior engineer, a technical lead, a principal data scientist, or a "head of AI engineering" who reports to a member of the coalition, who is tasked with the content the coalition cannot specify, and who is evaluated by the coalition in Form Metrics. The absorber absorbs the gap. The absorber does the seeing the coalition cannot do, and the coalition retains the authority to decide what to do with what the absorber sees.

The Absorber Hire has a predictable trajectory. The absorber enters with contributory expertise and discovers, within a quarter, that the role's content was never specified because the specifiers could not specify it. The absorber specifies it. The absorber builds the thing. The absorber presents the thing to the coalition, which evaluates its form — the deck, the timeline, the stakeholder alignment, the governance status — and cannot evaluate its content. The absorber's contributory judgment, where it conflicts with the coalition's best practice or borrowed horizon, is overruled, because the coalition holds authority and the absorber holds only expertise. The absorber either learns to present in Form Metrics and stops exercising the judgment they were hired for — becoming, functionally, a member of the ceremony — or continues to exercise it, accumulates a reputation for being difficult, and is managed out under the available vehicle: not a team player, too in the weeds, not strategic enough, uncomfortable with ambiguity.

The coalition experiences the absorber's departure as confirmation. The technical person did not work out. Technical people often do not, at this level. What the role needs is someone more strategic. The posting is rewritten, the clause is strengthened, and the next absorber is hired.

## IX. Managerial Debt

Engineers have a name for the cost of decisions made under pressure that defer a problem rather than solve it: technical debt. The debt is not the decision; it is the interest the decision accrues, paid by everyone who subsequently works in the codebase the decision shaped, until someone pays the principal by rebuilding what the decision built wrong.

Managerial Debt is the analogous quantity for decisions made above the ceiling. Every decision about the work made by someone who cannot evaluate the work — which workflow to automate, which vendor to standardize on, which framework to impose, which metric to target, which absorber to hire and which to manage out — carries a probability of being wrong that the decider cannot estimate. The wrong decisions are not corrected, because the correction would require the evaluation the decider cannot perform. They are built on. The engineers below the ceiling work inside the consequences of the decision, pay the interest in capacity diverted to accommodating it, and are not positioned to retire the principal because the decision's author holds the authority to change it and cannot see why it should be changed.

Managerial Debt has properties that distinguish it from its technical counterpart.

It is invisible to the debtor. Technical debt is at least visible to the engineers who incur it; they know the shortcut was a shortcut. Managerial Debt is incurred by someone who does not know the decision was wrong, because the knowledge would require contributory expertise. The decision looks, from above the ceiling, like a reasonable decision made on the best available information. The information was Form Metrics, best practice, and the borrowed horizon, and it was the best available to someone who could not read anything else.

It compounds through the coalition. A single manager's wrong decision is a local cost. A Gap Coalition's wrong decision — a sector-wide best practice, a shared vendor standard, a common governance framework — is a cost replicated across every organization in the coalition's network, validated by mimetic isomorphism, and immune to local correction because the local correction would deviate from the consensus.

It is paid at the ceiling. The interest on Managerial Debt is paid by the layer immediately beneath the ceiling: the absorbers, the senior engineers, the people with contributory expertise who must build inside the consequences of decisions they could see were wrong. The payment takes the form of capacity: time spent working around the imposed framework, re-translating probabilistic work into deterministic story points, building what the vendor roadmap demanded rather than what the workflow needed, documenting for governance that cannot read the documentation. The payment is invisible above the ceiling because it does not appear in any Form Metric. It appears, if anywhere, in attrition — the absorbers leave — and attrition is attributed, above the ceiling, to market conditions.

It accrues fastest under regime change. In a stable regime the decisions above the ceiling are mostly correct, because the manager's stale contributory expertise still approximately covers the work. Under regime change the coverage collapses and the error rate rises, and because the decisions are being made at the same cadence as before — the transformation office has a quarterly plan — the debt accrues at a rate the organization has never experienced and has no instrument to measure.

The principal on Managerial Debt is retired in only one way: by placing someone with contributory expertise in a position of authority over the decisions, and letting them rebuild what was built wrong. This is the move the Gap Coalition exists to prevent. The coalition therefore guarantees that the debt is never retired, only serviced — serviced by the absorbers, at the ceiling, until they leave, and then by their replacements.

## X. Managerial Senescence: The Altitude Drift

Everything above has been structural. This section is temporal. The question is what happens to a single manager across a career, and why the structure described above is not an aberration of a few bad promotions but the terminal state toward which the managerial career path tends.

A manager's career has an altitude. It begins near the work: the first-line supervisor is a practitioner with a team, still doing some of the work, still able to evaluate all of it. Each promotion raises the altitude and, following Katz, reduces the proportion of the manager's attention that goes to the work itself. By the third or fourth promotion the manager's contact with the work is entirely mediated: through reports, through Form Metrics, through the ceremonies of whatever stack the organization runs. The manager's contributory expertise, no longer exercised, decays. Their interactional expertise, exercised daily, sharpens. The ratio shifts, and with each year the manager becomes more fluent about the work and less able to do it.

This is the Altitude Drift, and it is the ordinary trajectory of a managerial career. It is not pathological in a stable regime. The manager at altitude governs through structures that embed the contributory expertise they no longer have, and the structures are adequate because the work has not changed in kind since the expertise was embedded.

The drift becomes senescence when the work changes in kind. Managerial Senescence is the condition of a manager whose contributory expertise has decayed through altitude, whose interactional expertise was built on a regime that has passed, and whose remaining capacity is the governance of ceremony. The senescent manager is not incompetent. They are competent at a thing — the form of the work — that has become disconnected from the thing that matters. Sørensen and Stuart's (2000) finding about aged firms, that they innovate more and less relevantly, has an individual analog: the senescent manager governs more and less relevantly, producing ever more structure around work they can see ever less of.

The senescent manager's defining behavior is the one the title names: they go closer to just managing work. Not the work — work. The abstraction. The manager at this altitude manages the existence, flow, and status of work as a category, and the category is the same whether the work is a procurement system in 2015 or an agentic workflow in 2026. The stack makes this possible. SAFe's release train does not care what it carries. The intake funnel does not care what it funnels. The governance review does not care what it reviews. The senescent manager operates the stack, and the stack is indifferent to content, and the indifference is what allows the manager to continue operating at an altitude from which content is invisible.

The certifications stack as the drift proceeds. Each promotion away from the work is accompanied by a credential in the governance of work: the manager leaves the engineering role and certifies as a scrum master, leaves the scrum-master role and certifies in scaled agile, leaves the program role and certifies in product operating models, enters the transformation role and certifies in AI leadership. The credentials are the manager's investment in interactional expertise, and the investment is rational: it is the only investment available to someone whose contributory expertise is no longer exercised. But the credentials are also the measure of the drift. A manager with four governance certifications and no technical credential of the last five years has a visible trajectory, and the trajectory points away from the thing they govern.

The senescent manager has a final structural feature that completes the picture. They cannot see their own senescence, for the reason the Interactional Ceiling is invisible from above: the test of whether one can still evaluate the work is a test only contributory expertise can administer. What the senescent manager experiences is confidence. The ceremonies run. The metrics are green. The coalition agrees. The best practices are followed. The vendor's roadmap says the division is on the right path. Every signal the manager can read says the transformation is proceeding, and the signals the manager cannot read — the absorber's private assessment, the engineers' translation tax, the workflow that passed governance and does not work — are not signals to them at all.

The organization that is full of senescent managers at altitude, under a regime change, is the organization the first document in this pair described: aging on the second clock while its first clock reports health. The Ceremonial Stack is the organizational mechanism. Managerial Senescence is the individual one. They are the same phenomenon at two scales, and they reinforce: the senescent manager builds the stack, the stack permits the manager to remain senescent, and the coalition ensures that no one inside the loop can see it as a loop.

## XI. The Formal Account

The framework's constructs admit a compact formalization whose purpose is to make the predictions falsifiable.

**Expertise decomposition.** For a manager m and domain d, let C(m, d) ∈ [0, 1] be contributory expertise and I(m, d) ∈ [0, 1] be interactional expertise. Evaluative capacity — the ability to tell good work from bad — is a function of C alone:

    V(m, d) = g(C(m, d)),  g increasing, g(0) = 0

Interactional expertise contributes to V nothing. It contributes to apparent capacity — what the manager's superiors and peers perceive — in proportion to their own inability to distinguish the two:

    V̂(m, d) = g(C) + h(I) · [1 − C(s, d)]

where s is the evaluating superior and h is increasing. When the superior's contributory expertise is zero, apparent capacity is driven entirely by fluency. This is the condition above the ceiling.

**The Interactional Ceiling.** Index organizational levels ℓ = 1 (practitioner) to L (top). The ceiling is

    ℓ* = max{ ℓ : C(m, d) ≥ c_min for some m at level ℓ }

the highest level at which anyone holds contributory expertise above a threshold sufficient for evaluation. Under regime change from d to d′, C(m, d′) ≪ C(m, d) for incumbents, and ℓ* falls. The ceiling's drop is proportional to the distance between regimes and inversely proportional to the organization's rate of promoting contributory experts in d′ — which the Gap Coalition suppresses.

**The Altitude Drift.** For a single manager, contributory expertise decays with time at altitude:

    dC/dt = −δ · ℓ(t)

with δ the decay rate and ℓ(t) the manager's level. Interactional expertise accumulates:

    dI/dt = +ι · ℓ(t)

A manager's career path raises ℓ(t) and therefore accelerates both the decay of C and the growth of I. The crossover point at which I > C is the Altitude Drift's inflection; Managerial Senescence is the regime where C has decayed below c_min and I continues to rise.

**The Ceremonial Stack.** Let the work have form F and content K. A governance structure G is content-blind if its output depends on F alone: G(F, K) = G(F, K′) for all K, K′. The Ceremonial Stack is the composition of content-blind structures. Its Ceremonial Coverage is the share of the manager's governance activity routed through content-blind structures:

    CC(m) = |{ decisions routed through G }| / |{ all decisions about d }|

A manager with C(m, d) < c_min must have CC → 1, because every decision not routed through a content-blind structure would require the C they lack. Ceremonial Coverage is therefore a direct observable of contributory deficit.

**Form Metrics.** A metric μ is a Form Metric if its correlation with the work's content value ρ(μ, K) is undefined or unmeasured at adoption and μ was selected for legibility to evaluators with C < c_min. The decoupling

    D(μ) = 1 − |ρ(μ, K)|

is present at adoption rather than induced by targeting; targeting raises it further.

**Ambiguity Externalization.** Let S_F be the specifiability of a role's form and S_K the specifiability of its content, each in [0, 1]. The Ambiguity Clause appears when

    S_F − S_K > α*

for some threshold α*: the role is fully specified in form and unspecified in content, and the gap is exported. The Ambiguity Export coefficient of a posting is

    AE = (S_F − S_K) · 𝟙[clause present]

High AE marks a role beneath the ceiling whose content the poster cannot see.

**The Gap Coalition.** For a set M of managers at a level above ℓ*, define the coalition's stability as the probability that a hiring or promotion decision made by M installs a contributory expert at M's level:

    P(install C ≥ c_min at level of M) = p_h · (1 − s_T) · (1 − s_I)

where p_h is the homophily-adjusted base rate (low, since contributory experts do not resemble M), s_T ∈ [0, 1] is the SEM threat suppression (the degree to which M's members discount a candidate whose C exceeds their own), and s_I is the collective-insurance suppression. All three terms push the probability toward zero. The coalition is stable when P is below the rate at which the ceiling would need to rise to track regime change.

**The Absorber Hire.** An absorber a is installed at level ℓ_a < ℓ* ≤ ℓ_M with C(a, d′) ≥ c_min, reporting to m ∈ M. The absorber's effective authority over content decisions is

    A_eff(a) = A(a) · [1 − override(m)]

where override(m) is the rate at which m overrules a's content judgments in favor of best practice or the borrowed horizon. Since m cannot evaluate a's judgments, override(m) is driven by conflict with external authority rather than by merit, and A_eff is low. The absorber's expected tenure falls with override(m), and the coalition reads the departure as confirmation.

**Managerial Debt.** Let each content decision by m carry an error probability e(m) = 1 − V(m, d′). Debt accrues as

    MD(T) = ∫₀ᵀ r(t) · e(m) · ξ(t) dt

where r(t) is the decision rate and ξ(t) the downstream cost per wrong decision. Interest is paid at level ℓ* as diverted capacity; principal is retired only by raising ℓ* above the decision's level, which the coalition prevents. Under regime change, e(m) steps up while r(t) is held constant by the transformation cadence, and MD accrues at a rate the organization has no Form Metric to observe.

The formal account's purpose is to make three things precise. First, that evaluative capacity depends on contributory expertise alone, and that fluency substitutes for it only in the perception of those who also lack it. Second, that the Ceremonial Stack, the Ambiguity Clause, and the Gap Coalition are each direct observables of a contributory deficit, not independent phenomena. Third, that the deficit is self-maintaining: the coalition prevents the one move — raising the ceiling — that would retire the debt it accrues.

## XIII. Predictions

Each prediction names what would be seen if the framework is right and what would be seen if it is wrong.

**P-CS-1 — The Ambiguity Clause's frequency tracks the contributory gap, not the work.** Across postings for AI transformation roles at large institutions, the presence of the clause will correlate with the absence of a content specification (no named deliverable, no technical constraint, no success criterion beyond stakeholder alignment) and will not correlate with any independent measure of the role's actual technical uncertainty. Postings written by or reviewed by contributory experts will contain the clause at a measurably lower rate and a content specification at a measurably higher one. Falsifier: the clause appears at equal rates regardless of who authored the posting and regardless of content specificity.

**P-CS-2 — Certification stacking predicts governance-to-content ratio.** A manager's count of process and governance certifications acquired in the preceding five years, net of technical credentials, will predict their Ceremonial Coverage: the share of their decisions routed through content-blind structures. Managers with four or more governance credentials and zero recent technical credentials will show Ceremonial Coverage approaching one. Falsifier: certification mix is uncorrelated with governance-to-content ratio, or heavily credentialed managers are found to make content decisions at rates equal to those of practitioners.

**P-CS-3 — Absorber tenure is shorter than peer tenure and shortens with override rate.** Contributory experts hired into AI transformation functions beneath coalition managers will show median tenure measurably shorter than peers hired into the same functions without contributory expertise, and their tenure will fall as the rate at which their content judgments are overruled in favor of best practice or vendor roadmap rises. Exit will be attributed, in the organization's own record, to fit, strategic orientation, or comfort with ambiguity rather than to any content disagreement. Falsifier: absorber tenure matches or exceeds peer tenure, or exits are documented as content disagreements.

**P-CS-4 — The coalition's consensus is detectable as vocabulary convergence without content convergence.** Senior AI transformation leaders across a sector will show high convergence in vocabulary (the same frameworks, the same vendor language, the same best-practice phrases) and low convergence in content (what has actually been built, what it does, what it cost), with the content question routinely answered in Form Metrics. Falsifier: leaders who share vocabulary also share and can articulate specific, comparable content outcomes.

**P-CS-5 — The AI ceremony layer will churn faster than the stack beneath it.** The AI-specific layer of the Ceremonial Stack — intake forms, readiness assessments, champion networks, prompt libraries, agent governance frameworks — will be replaced or renamed at a rate exceeding one cycle per year, as each codified best practice is superseded by the next. The process framework beneath it (the agile or scaled-agile base) will persist unchanged. The churn will be presented as learning. Falsifier: the AI ceremony layer stabilizes within two years into a durable form that the practitioners beneath the ceiling independently endorse as fitting the work.

**P-CS-6 — Form Metrics will show green while content measures, where they exist, show flat.** At institutions reporting transformation progress in intake volume, template completion, training rates, and login-based adoption, independent content measures — workflows in production producing measurable value at acceptable cost, as assessed by practitioners — will show substantially lower progress, and the gap will not be reported upward because no one above the ceiling can specify the content measure. Falsifier: Form Metric progress and practitioner-assessed content progress move together.

**P-CS-7 — Managerial Debt's interest will appear as absorber attrition, and attrition will be attributed to the market.** The layer immediately beneath the Interactional Ceiling in AI transformation functions will show attrition exceeding that of the layer above and the layer below, and the organization's own account of the attrition will cite talent-market tightness or compensation rather than the governance conditions under which the layer worked. Falsifier: attrition is uniform across layers, or the organization's account identifies governance conditions as a cause.

**P-CS-8 — The Katz Inversion will be visible in the failure pattern of senior hires.** Externally hired senior AI transformation executives selected on conceptual and communication skill without contributory assessment will show measurably shorter tenure and lower practitioner-rated effectiveness than those selected with a contributory assessment, and the institutions making the first kind of hire will continue making it, because the assessment that would reveal the pattern is itself a content judgment above the ceiling. Falsifier: tenure and effectiveness are uncorrelated with whether contributory expertise was assessed at hire.

**P-CS-9 — Raising the ceiling will occur, where it occurs, through crisis rather than design.** The first large institutions to place contributory experts in authority over AI transformation decisions will do so following a visible failure — a workflow that passed governance and caused a loss, a vendor standardization that had to be reversed, a regulatory finding — rather than through deliberate restructuring of the promotion and hiring machinery. The coalition's stability condition predicts that design-driven ceiling raises will be rare. Falsifier: a meaningful number of large institutions restructure their transformation leadership toward contributory expertise absent a precipitating failure.

**P-CS-10 — The Best-Practice Shield will lag the frontier by a measurable interval that widens.** The practices invoked as "best" in large-institution AI governance documents will, on examination, correspond to the sector consensus of a prior period, and the interval between a practice's codification and its invocation as best will widen as the transition accelerates — from quarters toward years — while the invokers' confidence in the practice's currency remains constant. Falsifier: invoked best practices track the frontier within a stable, short interval.

**P-CS-11 — Senescent managers will describe the transition in the vocabulary of the prior one.** Managers whose contributory expertise was built in a prior regime will, when describing the AI transition, use the structural vocabulary of that regime — workstreams, releases, rollouts, change management, adoption curves — and will apply its cadence and decomposition to work that does not have them. The practitioners beneath them will use a different vocabulary, and the gap between the two vocabularies within a single function will be a measurable marker of the ceiling's location. Falsifier: vocabulary is shared across the ceiling, or senescent managers adopt practitioner vocabulary with corresponding changes in decision content.

## XV. The Frame

The executive director has a new title, a new floor, and a transformation to run. They have eleven years of delivering things that could be seen, four certifications in the governance of things that cannot, and a quarterly plan. The plan has an intake funnel, a readiness assessment, a champions network, a governance board, and a dashboard. The dashboard is green.

Beneath the executive director, two levels down, there is an engineer who was hired to be comfortable with ambiguity. The engineer has built the thing the division actually needed — not the thing on the roadmap the vendor supplied, and not the thing the intake funnel prioritized, but the thing that works. The engineer presented it. The deck was adequate; the engineer is not a presenter. The governance board asked whether it had been through the responsible-AI review. It had. The board asked about the timeline. The timeline was fine. The board did not ask whether it worked, because no one on the board could have evaluated the answer, and so no one thought to ask the question.

The executive director's peers — the VPs in the adjacent transformation functions, the other executive directors, the managing director who sponsors them all — meet monthly. They speak the same language. They cite the same practices. They have the same dashboards and the same vendors and the same four certifications. They find one another credible, and they find the engineer, when the engineer's name comes up, a little difficult. Too in the weeds. Not quite strategic. Perhaps not comfortable with the ambiguity after all.

None of them is doing anything wrong. The executive director was promoted by a system that measured what it could see. The certifications were the investment the system recommended. The best practices are what the sector does. The vendor roadmap is what the vendor said. The governance board governs what governance boards govern. The dashboard reports what dashboards report. Every element is sound, and the elements stack, and the stack sits on top of the work like a control panel on a machine no one in the room can open.

The machine is the strategy now. That is what changed. For seventy years the people at altitude could manage the panel because the machine beneath it was stable, and understanding it was a junior skill, and growing out of it was development. The machine is no longer stable. It is being rebuilt, and the rebuilding is the only decision that matters, and the decision is being made by people who were told, at every stage of their careers, that the ability to make it was something to leave behind.

They left it behind. They were rewarded for leaving it. The reward is the title, the floor, the transformation, the plan. The ceiling runs just beneath their feet. They cannot see it, because the only instrument that detects it is the one they set down eleven years ago, on the advice of a model written for a world where the machine did not move.

The engineer will leave in the spring. The posting will go up in the summer. It will ask for someone comfortable with ambiguity.
